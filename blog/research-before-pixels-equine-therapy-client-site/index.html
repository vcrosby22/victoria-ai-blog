Research Before Pixels: Building an Equine Therapy Site With Real Clients

The clients loved the demo — but the win started three weeks earlier with competitor research, a discovery agenda, and the simple trust patterns nonprofits actually need.

#nonprofit#client-work#ux-research#equine-therapy#discovery

The clients saw the site and loved it. That was the payoff of a multi-session arc — but the moment that mattered most happened before I opened a code editor.

I was building a website for Center X Equine Therapy, a 501(c)(3) nonprofit offering physical and occupational therapy through horses. They wanted something polished, warm, and trustworthy — modeled loosely on The Shea Center, a well-regarded therapeutic riding program in Orange County.

The live preview is at center-x-equine-therapy-site.pages.dev/programs. The story of how it got there is mostly research, planning, and small decisions that are easy to skip when you’re excited to ship.

The simple stuff that gets overlooked

When I started, I didn’t wireframe. I studied what already works.

I pulled reference sites from San Diego and Orange County — Ride Above Disability, Shea Center, Tara’s Chance, SD Therapeutic Horsemanship — and captured the patterns that showed up again and again:

PatternWhy it matters
Emotional hero + one mission lineFamilies and donors understand purpose in five seconds
Three CTAs: Donate, Volunteer, Get startedMatches how nonprofits actually convert
Parent quotes with namesTrust beats generic “we help kids” copy
PATH Intl (or equivalent) badgeIndustry trust signal for therapeutic riding
”What is equine therapy?” explainerMany visitors are new to the concept
Meet the horses + sponsorshipEmotional connection and recurring donation hook

None of this is glamorous. All of it shows up on the sites that convert.

I archived the research in a dedicated project folder — separate from my general design knowledge base — so the vertical context stays isolated and reusable without polluting generic UI guidance.

Discovery before build

Before the first HTML file, I ran a structured client meeting with a discovery agenda: programs, bios, fundraising setup, social channels, waitlist needs, and deployment path.

The meeting surfaced concrete constraints:

  • Two therapist bios and two horse bios — real content, not lorem ipsum
  • Shea Center as the design north star — large hero imagery, possibly video later
  • Domain already registered at GoDaddy (centerxequinetherapy.org), currently a “Launching Soon” page
  • PayPal for donations today; platform options still open
  • Instagram required; YouTube maybe
  • Waitlist tooling undecided — Google Sheets, Airtable, or embedded forms

That last point mattered more than it sounds. Families visiting the waitlist page need to understand who the service is for, who they’ll work with, what joining the waitlist means, and when to expect a response. Without that trust content near the conversion point, a pretty form is just a pretty form.

Planning to prevent overbuilding

After the client meeting, I spent a full planning day separating concerns that usually get tangled:

Design direction — Shea-inspired polish, but stronger fundraising UX and accessibility by default.

HIPAA boundary — The public website is a marketing and fundraising site. It should not collect protected health information. The waitlist form is a modular piece that can swap to a HIPAA-capable vendor later without rebuilding the site.

CRM readiness — Even before picking Airtable or Sheets, we structured form categories, source tags, and follow-up statuses so future integration isn’t a rewrite.

Repo ownership — Client code lives under a ProgramEdgeLLC GitHub organization, not my personal account. Clean handoff, professional container.

The insight that stuck: the right architecture for small healthcare-adjacent nonprofit sites is often a strong public marketing site plus a clean handoff to secure intake — not a HIPAA-heavy custom build from day one.

What we actually shipped

The build itself was static HTML, CSS, and vanilla JavaScript — mobile-first, accessibility-checked, real client photos in the hero carousel, therapist bios with credential sections styled for scanability, and a high-fidelity donation mockup ready for Givebutter once the client’s EIN is confirmed.

Polish rounds happened live — Victoria reviewing on her iPhone, iterating the mobile menu overlay from a cramped brown dropdown to a full-screen green vignette with backdrop blur. Hero images swapped from soft web downloads to sharp, face-forward client originals: volunteers with the palomino, a toddler in session with a therapist.

The clients loved it. GoDaddy access landed the same week. The site became the live embedded demo on program-edge.com — client work and studio portfolio reinforcing each other.

What I’d do differently

I’d write the reusable client-site intake checklist earlier. The discovery agenda worked; having it as a template from project one would have saved an hour of formatting.

I’d also flag the mailto: reliability issue before any footer shipped — primary CTAs should be real URLs; email works better as copy-able text with an optional mailto fallback.
