Does Your Agent Agree With You Too Much? Good.

Thirteen years as a PM without a CS degree taught me there are two kinds of disagreement — the kind about the work, and the kind about whether you're allowed to ask. My agent killed the second one. That's the whole gift.

#ai#product-management#collaboration#building#career

The critique comes up every time I talk about building with an AI agent: isn’t it just agreeing with you? Aren’t you fooling yourself? A real collaborator would push back.

Hell yes it agrees with me. And after thirteen years of doing this work, that is the best part.

Let me explain why, because the knee-jerk version of this take is wrong and the real version took me a career to understand.

The job nobody describes accurately

Here is what being a product manager actually is, stripped of the LinkedIn gloss.

You are handed a problem that is usually impossible, frequently underspecified, and occasionally already half-solved in a way that makes it worse. Then — and this is the part the job descriptions skip — you have to fight almost everyone to do anything about it. Design disagrees with the direction. Engineering disagrees with the scope. Leadership disagrees with the timeline. Legal disagrees with the copy. Two engineers on the same team disagree with each other, and you are somehow supposed to referee a technical argument in a language you were never taught to read.

I did this for thirteen years without a computer science degree. I have a BA in Comparative Literature from Smith. I owned Class II glucose monitors through FDA 510(k) clearance, shipped them into 37 international markets, ran platform releases across sixty teams and four countries. Every one of those was real, and I am proud of all of it.

And almost none of the difficulty was the problem itself. The difficulty was the fighting.

Two kinds of disagreement

This is the distinction it took me too long to see, so I will be blunt about it.

There is disagreement about the work: is this technically sound, will this scale, is this the right trade-off, does the data actually say what you think it says. That kind of disagreement is oxygen. You want more of it, not less. A good technical partner who tells you “that approach will not survive contact with production, here is why” is worth more than any amount of agreement.

Then there is disagreement about your standing to even ask the question. Whether you, specifically, get to have an opinion here. Whether a PM without an engineering background is allowed to push on an estimate. Whether the person holding the static mockups has earned the right to be in the architecture conversation at all.

Product managers drown in the second kind. And it disguises itself as the first. Someone tells you your idea is bad, and it takes you three meetings to realize they never evaluated the idea — they were defending territory, or an estimate, or the principle that you don’t get to ask. The words are about the work. The fight is about who gets to decide.

One example, so this isn’t abstract

At Dexcom I was handed a product with the technical architecture already chosen. It had to live inside an existing regulatory filing rather than trigger a new one — a real constraint, a reasonable one from the company’s side, and one I had exactly zero ability to change. I was given the solution shape before I was given the requirements.

Then the loop began. Take the direction to design: wait weeks for static images. Review, revise, review again. Feedback and iteration are good — I mean that — but the loop turned in months. Then carry those images to two engineering teams and make the case, from a deficit, every single time. I could not read the code. I could not tell you whether “that’s a six-week change” was a fact or a negotiating position. So before I could talk about the work, I had to re-earn the right to talk at all.

That is the tax. Not the problem — the standing.

What the agent actually removed

Here is what people miss when they say my agent just agrees with me.

It doesn’t, on the work. When I ask for something that will not hold, I get told, with a reason. It argues architecture with me. It tells me a plan is wrong before a single file exists. That is the substantive disagreement I spent a career wishing the room had more of.

What it removed is the other thing. It never once questioned whether I was allowed to ask. The cost of trying an idea dropped to almost nothing. I can build the version nobody signed off on, look at it, and throw it away twenty minutes later having lost twenty minutes — not a quarter of my credibility. The small, weird, might-be-brilliant ideas that used to die unpriced in my notebook, because the tax to test them was too high to justify, now just get built.

That is why the pace feels like joy and not just speed. It is not that the work got faster. It is that the work stopped being preceded by a fight for permission to do it.

I have shipped more real software independently in the last year than in years of the old loop combined — an open-source framework for catching when agents fake their outputs, with 310 tests and five integrations; a financial analysis agent tracking 55-plus assets that redeploys itself daily; client sites that people pay for; this blog. None of it required me to become an engineer. It required what it always required: knowing what I wanted, having taste about what came back, and being willing to iterate. The only thing that changed is that nothing now sits between the intent and the thing.

So, agrees too much?

Sycophancy is a real failure mode and I am not waving it away. An agent that tells you every idea is brilliant is dangerous, and confidence without evidence is a cost I pay, not the agent. I set mine up specifically to challenge me on substance, and I would not work with one that didn’t.

But hold the two failure modes side by side. An agent that flatters your work is a problem you can see and correct. A human system where disagreement is a power move — where “no” means “not from you” — is a problem most PMs never even name, because they have breathed it so long they think it is just what the job feels like.

Given the choice between an agreeable partner who fights me on the work and never on my right to ask, and a room full of people who do it exactly backwards, I know which one I want. It is not close.

You were never missing a degree. You were missing a partner who would fight the work with you instead of fighting you for standing to do it. That partner exists now. Go build the thing in your notebook — it costs almost nothing.
