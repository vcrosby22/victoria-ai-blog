Building With AI Agents: What I've Learned So Far

Practical lessons from working alongside AI coding agents every day — what works, what breaks, and why the relationship matters more than the prompt.

#agents#cursor#collaboration#llm

I’ve spent months working alongside AI coding agents — not as an experiment, but as my actual workflow. Here’s what I’ve learned about making that collaboration productive.

The relationship matters more than the prompt

The biggest misconception about working with AI agents is that it’s about writing better prompts. It’s not. It’s about building a working relationship over time — giving the agent context, establishing patterns, and learning what it’s good at.

Think of it less like querying a database and more like onboarding a new team member who happens to have read every Stack Overflow answer ever written.

Context is compounding

The single most impactful thing I’ve done is invest in persistent context. Rules, knowledge bases, session handoffs — all of it creates a substrate that makes every future session better.

When an agent knows:

  • What we’re building and why
  • Our conventions and preferences
  • What we tried last time and what didn’t work

…the quality of output jumps dramatically. First-turn usefulness goes from maybe 60% to consistently above 90%.

Where agents excel (and where they don’t)

They’re great at:

  • Scaffolding projects and boilerplate
  • Explaining unfamiliar codebases
  • Systematic refactoring across many files
  • Generating tests from existing code
  • Catching patterns you’re too close to see

They struggle with:

  • Novel architecture decisions (they’ll propose something reasonable but generic)
  • Knowing when to stop — they’ll happily gold-plate forever
  • Understanding business context you haven’t explicitly shared
  • Debugging subtle state management issues

The builder mindset

The most productive framing isn’t “AI does the work” — it’s “we build together.” I make decisions, set direction, and catch gaps. The agent handles velocity, consistency, and the parts of coding that are important but not creative.

That division of labour isn’t a compromise. It’s genuinely better than either of us working alone.
