Headless CMS, in Plain English: For Anyone Stuck Inside Their Website Templates

If your website lives on a templated platform and you have ever wished you could change more, or take your content with you, this is the idea worth knowing. A plain-language look at headless CMS, who it is for, the trade-offs, and the main tools, written while I am still learning it myself.

#headless-cms#small-business#re-platforming#sanity#astro#web

I have spent the last few weeks deep in something I did not know much about before: moving a real website off a closed, templated platform and onto what people call a “headless” setup. I am still learning it. This post is me writing down what has actually clicked, in the plainest language I can, for anyone who runs a small business or a personal site and has ever felt boxed in by their website tools.

The thing most website platforms quietly do

Most website builders, think Squarespace, Wix, and a lot of industry-specific platforms, bundle two very different jobs into one product:

  1. The editor you log into to change words and images.
  2. The templates that decide how the site can look.

They are welded together. That is why you can only arrange things the way the template allows, and why a truly custom design usually is not possible. It is also why, when you outgrow the platform, leaving is painful. Your content lives inside their system, and sometimes you cannot even export your own images at full quality.

For a lot of people this is completely fine. If a template does what you need, you should stay on it. Simple is a feature.

What “headless” actually means

A headless CMS splits those two jobs apart.

  • One system stores and edits your content (your text, images, products, artworks) as plain structured data. This is the “CMS.”
  • A separate system turns that content into the actual website, with a design that can be anything you build.

“Headless” just means the content system has no website of its own, no fixed “head.” It hands your content to whatever front-end you point at it.

Because the two are separate, two good things happen at once: the design is completely yours, and you still get a friendly screen to edit content. And because your content sits in its own system, you own it and can take it with you.

The honest trade-off: a headless setup takes real work to build the first time, and edits are not always instant (many setups rebuild the site in about a minute after you publish). For a gallery, a studio, or a small brand, that is a non-issue. For a breaking-news site, it might matter.

The pieces, without the jargon

A headless site is usually three layers:

  • The content layer (the CMS): where you and your team edit. Common names: Sanity, Contentful, Strapi, Storyblok, Kontent.ai, Prismic, Hygraph, Payload. They differ mostly in who they are built for. Storyblok leans toward marketers who want visual editing; Strapi is the popular open-source, self-hosted option; Contentful sits in the enterprise world; Sanity is loved for flexibility.
  • The build layer (the front-end): a tool that turns your content plus your design into finished pages ahead of time. Astro and Next.js are common choices.
  • The host: where the finished site lives and is served fast worldwide. Cloudflare Pages, Vercel, Netlify.

You do not have to know all of these. You mostly need to know they exist, and that the combination is what gives you a custom, fast, portable site without hand-building a database and a login screen from scratch.

This is not a niche idea

While I was learning this, I was a little surprised how much momentum is behind it. Analysts sizing the headless CMS market land in different places, from roughly one billion to a few billion dollars in 2025 depending on how they count, but they agree on the direction: it is growing on the order of twenty percent a year, heading toward the low tens of billions within about a decade. In plain terms, more and more teams are choosing to own their stack rather than rent a template.

Should you care?

If your template does everything you need, genuinely, stay put. But if you have ever thought “I wish I could change this and I can’t,” or “I’m nervous I can’t get my own content out,” that feeling is worth taking seriously. That is usually the moment a headless setup starts to make sense.

I am writing this as someone still in the middle of learning it, not from a mountaintop. That is honestly one of my favorite parts of building with AI right now: I can pick up a whole area like this quickly, test it on something real, and share what I find while it is still fresh. If you are weighing a re-platform, or you just want to understand the option, I hope this made it a little clearer.
